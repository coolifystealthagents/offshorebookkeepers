## Paper leaves inventory before a sale exists

Commercial printers issue sheets or rolls to jobs before finished quantities are known. Some material is expected for press setup, color calibration, and finishing tests. Other material is lost to jams, registration problems, customer changes, or reprints. If all issued paper becomes job cost without a quantity bridge, purchasing sees unexplained shrinkage and estimators cannot distinguish an approved allowance from an unusual production event.

Use the job ticket as the backbone. Record customer, job, version, planned quantity, overs allowance, stock code, sheet or roll dimensions, basis weight, warehouse lot, press, planned setup quantity, issue quantity, returns, finished good count, scrap quantity, and approved cause. Keep the original estimate and later revisions with effective dates. A revised ticket should not erase what the floor received under the first version.

## Agree the unit of measure before comparing totals

Purchasing may buy pounds or rolls, inventory may issue sheets, and production may report impressions. Create documented conversions for each stock and size, including assumptions and rounding. Do not silently convert a partial roll to a convenient sheet count. Where measurement is approximate, label the method and tolerance so the reviewer knows whether a difference is meaningful.

Freeze warehouse issues, production counts, finished quantities, return slips, and scrap records at the same cutoff. Save report filters and timestamps. A late return to inventory can make the warehouse month differ from the production close; record it as a cutoff item rather than changing the issue date. The bookkeeper preserves each source and builds the bridge.

## Build the job-material equation

For sheet work, begin with stock issued plus approved additional issues. Subtract usable stock returned, finished quantity adjusted for pieces per sheet, planned make-ready consumption, and measured unexpected spoilage. Any remainder is unexplained. For roll work, the bridge may use starting and ending length or weight and documented conversion. Keep planned and unexpected waste in separate columns even if both ultimately enter job cost under the approved policy.

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's [sustainable materials management resources](https://www.epa.gov/smm) provide broader waste context, and NIST's [Manufacturing Extension Partnership](https://www.nist.gov/mep) offers manufacturing resources. Those sources do not set a printer's inventory or costing policy. Production engineers and finance owners should establish standards suited to the equipment and processes.

## Test a reprint as a new event

A catalog job plans 20,000 finished pieces, four-up, with 600 setup sheets. The warehouse issues 5,800 sheets. Production reports 5,000 good sheets, 620 setup and calibration sheets, 130 sheets lost to a feeder problem, and 50 usable sheets returned. The quantities balance. Later, the customer approves a 1,000-piece reprint after a finishing defect. Open a linked reprint event with its own authorization, issues, counts, and cause; do not add material to the original job without explanation.

Suppose the reprint consumes 310 sheets for 250 good sheets, 40 setup sheets, and 20 damaged in finishing. The source balance is clear, but who bears the cost is a separate decision. The bookkeeper can present the material and labor evidence. Production management determines the cause, commercial management handles customer responsibility, and finance approves the accounting treatment.

## Make cause codes evidence-based

Useful causes include approved setup, registration, color, substrate defect, equipment interruption, finishing, customer revision, estimating variance, handling damage, test run, and unknown pending review. Require a note or work-order link for significant events. “Operator” should not be a catch-all. A cause code describes the authorized operational conclusion, not a payroll or disciplinary finding.

Review repeat events by press, stock, finishing step, and job type, but avoid claiming causation from bookkeeping data alone. Counts can change because measurement improved. Track the proportion of jobs balanced by cutoff, unresolved quantity, additional issues without ticket approval, and reprints missing authorization as workflow indicators.

## Reconcile inventory and value separately

First reconcile quantities by stock from opening inventory through purchases, issues, returns, adjustments, and closing count. Then apply approved costs to the supported job movements. A quantity tie can still carry the wrong unit cost, and a value tie can hide offsetting quantity errors. Review manual cost overrides, negative stock, old open jobs, and jobs marked complete with material still issued.

## Protect customer-supplied and certified stock

Customer-supplied paper should carry a separate ownership flag, receipt record, storage location, and authorized use. It may move through the same press but should not be absorbed into company inventory or valued as a purchase. At job completion, document finished use, spoilage, and any return or disposal instruction so the remaining quantity does not become anonymous warehouse stock.

Certified, recycled, or lot-controlled papers may require additional source records. Preserve supplier certificates or lot references where the approved customer or production process calls for them, and keep substitutions visible. The bookkeeper confirms that the specified evidence is attached; production and compliance owners decide whether a substitute meets the job requirement.

Count leftover offcuts only when the approved inventory method treats them as reusable stock. Record dimensions, stock, location, and quantity so a future issue can consume the specific remainder. Otherwise, labeling waste as inventory can postpone a real variance without creating material the press can use.

Archive job tickets, estimates, issue slips, return records, production counts, reprint approvals, cause decisions, and the inventory-to-ledger reconciliation.
