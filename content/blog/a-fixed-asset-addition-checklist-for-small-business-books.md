A studio buys a set of strength machines for 18,000 dollars. The bookkeeper records the payment as equipment expense, and the month's profit drops. The machines will last eight years, and the accounts now understate the asset and overstate the expense.

A fixed asset addition checklist keeps that from happening. It confirms the purchase belongs on the balance sheet, records the details, and starts depreciation from the right date.

## Confirm the asset test

Check the purchase against the client's capitalization policy: the amount, the useful life, and the category. A repair that extends the life of an asset may also be capitalizable.

Record the policy reference. A decision that is not tied to a policy will be revisited every time a similar purchase appears.

## Record the cost

Record the purchase price, the tax, the delivery, and the installation. The cost of bringing the asset into use is part of the asset.

Separate the cost from any training or service contract. A training invoice is an expense, even when it arrives with the equipment.

## Record the in-service date

The in-service date is when the asset is ready and available for use, not the invoice date or the payment date. Record it with the evidence.

The date drives the depreciation start. A wrong date shifts the expense and the tax position.

## Attach the evidence

Attach the invoice, the payment record, and the asset tag or photo. The evidence should let a reviewer identify the asset and the amount.

Record where the asset is located. A register that cannot be tied to a physical item is hard to audit.

## Set the useful life and method

Record the useful life, the method, and any salvage value under the client's policy. Do not choose a life to match a desired monthly expense.

Where the client follows tax depreciation, note the book and tax difference. The register should show both where the policy requires it.

## Update the depreciation schedule

Add the asset to the schedule and run the first month. Check that the monthly amount matches the expected calculation.

Record the reviewer and the date. An addition that is not in the schedule will be missing from the close.

## Review the addition at the close

Compare the fixed asset additions for the month with the capital expenditure report or the bank payments. A purchase that is missing is usually a coding error.

Record the reconciliation and the exceptions. The review is the control that catches the expense that should have been an asset.

## Threshold and policy

The capitalization threshold should be set by the client and applied consistently. A 500-dollar tool and a 5,000-dollar machine are treated differently under most policies.

Record the threshold and the category. A purchase near the threshold deserves a note, not a silent decision.

## Disposal and transfer

If an asset is sold, scrapped, or transferred, record the date and the proceeds. The depreciation schedule should stop at the disposal date.

A transfer between locations is not a disposal, but the register should show where the asset is. The physical count depends on it.

## Physical verification

A periodic count ties the register to the floor. Record the date, the items found, and the items missing.

A missing asset is a question for the client, not an automatic write-off. The note should show what was checked and who confirmed it.

## A worked example

A gym buys 12 machines for 24,000 dollars. The invoice includes delivery and installation, and the assets are placed in service on the first of the month.

The bookkeeper records the cost, the date, and a five-year life, and adds the assets to the schedule. The first month's depreciation is recorded, and the register ties to the invoice.

## Reporting

Report the additions, disposals, and depreciation for the month. A short summary with the counts and the amounts is enough for the client.

Record the reviewer and the date. The report is the evidence that the register was updated.

## What the checklist does not do

The checklist does not decide whether the purchase was a good investment, and it does not replace the client's tax advice. It records the asset consistently.

It also does not replace the physical verification. A register without a periodic count is a list that may not match the floor.
