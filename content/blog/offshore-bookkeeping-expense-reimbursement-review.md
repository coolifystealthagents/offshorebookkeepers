August 20, 2026 operating guide. This article is about bookkeeping preparation and does not interpret tax, employment, or reimbursement law. The client’s policy and approvers control the outcome.

## Define a complete claim
A claim should identify claimant, entity, date, amount, currency, business purpose, cost center or project, payment method, and receipt status. The offshore bookkeeper can check whether required fields are present and return an incomplete claim. Do not fill a missing business purpose from guesswork. A clean intake rule prevents the queue from becoming a place where evidence is reconstructed after payment.

## Check the receipt against the claim
Compare merchant, date, amount, currency, tax line where relevant, and description. Mark each check passed, missing, or inconsistent. A receipt can support an amount without proving business purpose. Keep source evidence linked and do not overwrite the submitted file. If a receipt is unreadable, request a replacement or use the client’s approved exception route rather than treating a partial image as sufficient.

## Separate coding from approval
The bookkeeper may suggest an account, department, or project code under a documented mapping. The claimant or responsible manager confirms the business purpose, and an authorized approver accepts the claim. A preparer should not approve a claim they submitted or clear their own exception. Store the coding note separately from the approval so a reviewer can see who made each decision.

## Apply policy as written
Use the current policy version, effective date, threshold, and exception route. Do not invent a rule because a claim looks unusual. If policy language is ambiguous, record the fact pattern and ask the policy owner. The offshore role can identify missing receipts, duplicate claims, out-of-period dates, or unsupported categories. It should not decide that a prohibited expense is acceptable because the amount is small.

## Screen for duplicates carefully
Search claimant, date, merchant, amount, receipt number, and prior submissions. Similar claims can be legitimate recurring costs, split receipts, or resubmissions after rejection. Label a candidate duplicate and retain the comparison. The finance owner decides whether to reject, combine, or investigate. Never delete a rejected claim solely to make the queue shorter; preserve disposition and reason.

## Route exceptions with a question
An escalation should state the claim identifier, evidence checked, policy paragraph or field missing, issue, requested decision, owner, and due date. Avoid “please advise” without context. If an approver returns the claim, record what must change and who will provide it. This keeps an offshore handoff useful across time zones and prevents an employee from receiving conflicting instructions from different channels.

## Reconcile approved claims
At cutoff, compare approved claims to the payment batch, employee clearing account, and ledger posting. Investigate approved claims not paid, payments without approved claims, duplicate payment references, and amounts that differ. The bookkeeper prepares the bridge. The finance owner decides corrections, hold releases, and any claim that requires a policy exception. Reconciliation should end with a dated status, not merely a balanced total.

## Protect personal information
Use the approved expense system and restricted folders. Avoid broad exports that include bank details, home addresses, or unnecessary personal identifiers. Named accounts, multifactor authentication, and limited download permissions reduce exposure. The role needs enough evidence to test a claim, not every employee record. Review access after a contractor or bookkeeper changes assignments.

## Measure quality and delay
Track first-pass completeness, return reasons, review age, duplicate candidates, exceptions by owner, approved-to-paid time, and rework. A fast approval count can conceal weak evidence if claims return after payment or require manual repair. Segment delays caused by claimant response, approver response, and bookkeeping processing. Use the result to improve the form and policy communication rather than to reward unsupported speed.

## Test boundary cases
Rehearse a complete claim, missing receipt, foreign currency, personal-looking charge, duplicate candidate, project expense, late submission, and policy exception. Have a second preparer follow the written steps. Confirm that each scenario reaches the correct owner and that no one can approve their own submission. Repair ambiguous codes and access before expanding volume.

## Practical conclusion

An expense packet is ready for closure when the employee, entity, period, amount, policy test, evidence, reviewer status, and accounting result are visible. If the receipt is missing, record the approved exception or the next request rather than substituting a guess. If the amount changed, retain the submitted amount and identify who approved the change. The offshore bookkeeper can update the register, reconcile the reimbursement to the payment, and report ageing; the authorized owner decides exceptions and final approval.

Review the queue for repeated causes. A high number of missing receipts may point to an intake problem, unclear policy, or a system limitation. Separate those operating observations from conclusions about employee behavior. Keep a summary of volume, returned items, unresolved days, and exception categories only when the definitions are stable.
An offshore bookkeeper can prepare reimbursement records effectively when completeness, receipt checks, coding, policy use, duplicate review, approval, payment, and reconciliation are separate visible steps. The support role documents facts and routes exceptions. Employees and authorized managers own purpose and approval; finance owners retain policy interpretation and correction decisions. A reviewer should be able to follow one claim from submission through final ledger evidence without relying on chat history.

## Keep the review rhythm simple
Run a daily intake check, a scheduled approval review, and a period-end reconciliation. Each rhythm needs a named owner and a clear population. The bookkeeper reports what is waiting, what is incomplete, and what has been approved but not paid. The finance lead decides which aged exceptions require intervention. Regular cadence is more reliable than a large cleanup immediately before close.
