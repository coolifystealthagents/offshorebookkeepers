## A return ticket is not a damage conclusion

When a lift, generator, camera, or compact excavator comes back, several financial events may follow: the rental closes, a deposit becomes refundable, a late charge arises, a technician documents damage, a repair vendor invoices parts, and the company decides whether a customer owes anything. Combining these events into one miscellaneous adjustment weakens both the customer conversation and the ledger. The return should begin a controlled case, not an automatic charge.

Create the case from the rental agreement number and asset ID. Add customer ID, location, dispatch and return times, meter readings, fuel level, accessories, original condition images, check-in images, inspector, and check-in timestamp. Link the contract version that was effective when the rental began. Never replace the first inspection with a cleaner later report. If the equipment is unsafe, operations handles the safety response; the bookkeeping record merely points to the authorized status.

## Split the financial streams

Use separate schedule columns for refundable deposit, rental revenue, damage-waiver charge, late or fuel charge, repair estimate, actual repair cost, customer damage charge, tax, insurance recovery, deposit application, cash refund, and open receivable. These amounts answer different questions. A waiver fee may be earned even when no damage occurs, while an actual repair cost may remain with the rental company depending on the contract and decision. The preparer must not infer one treatment from another.

Preserve gross activity in the reconciliation. Start with deposit cash received, show authorized applications and refunds, and arrive at the unapplied deposit balance. Separately roll repair work from estimate to vendor invoice and payment. Then roll any approved customer recovery from charge notice to invoice, credit, receipt, or write-off. A net $0 can conceal a $3,000 repair and a $3,000 deposit application, which is not a useful audit trail.

## Build evidence before the invoice

The case packet should contain the signed agreement, rate and waiver selections, dispatch condition report, return inspection, dated images, technician narrative, repair estimate, vendor invoice, internal labor calculation if permitted, communications, approval, customer invoice, credit memo, and deposit record. The [IRS recordkeeping guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/recordkeeping) supplies general background on supporting purchases and expenses. The [FTC business guidance library](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance) is a starting point for consumer-protection awareness, but the company should obtain appropriate advice for its agreements and jurisdiction.

Images need context. File names should connect to the asset, agreement, inspection stage, and time. A close-up of a broken panel without an asset identifier or “before” view may not establish when the damage arose. Keep ambiguous evidence labeled as ambiguous. Bookkeeping should never turn a technician's observation into a legal conclusion.

## Example: a deposit that does not settle the case

A contractor returns a mini excavator with a $2,500 deposit. The check-in records a cracked side cover. A vendor estimates $3,200, later invoices $2,760, and the rental manager approves a $2,100 customer charge after reviewing the agreement and prior photos. The schedule shows all four amounts: deposit held $2,500, actual repair $2,760, approved customer charge $2,100, and proposed refund $400. It does not simply post a $400 refund and $660 net repair cost.

If the customer disputes the charge before the refund is released, mark the deposit status under the approved policy and route the decision. Do not use the disputed $400 as an informal offset against another invoice. When a credit is approved, link it to the original case and preserve the earlier invoice. The reviewer can then reconstruct how the approved outcome changed.

## Exceptions should reflect the rental cycle

Useful statuses include return not inspected, meter mismatch, accessory missing, image incomplete, pre-existing condition unclear, estimate pending, contract question, waiver review, customer decision pending, vendor invoice pending, deposit refund due, recovery invoiced, disputed, and closed. Give every open case a next owner and date. Sort by both dollar exposure and time since return so modest deposits are not held indefinitely.

An offshore bookkeeper may gather records, compare identifiers, reperform the deposit arithmetic, update case status, and draft entries or customer-support schedules for review. The bookkeeper does not decide fault, coverage, safety, contract enforceability, customer liability, refund withholding, or write-off. Those decisions remain with authorized rental operations, finance, insurers, or advisers.

## Tie the case register to the books

At month-end, reconcile open deposits to the deposit liability and cash receipts; approved customer charges to invoices and receivables; actual repairs to work orders, payables, and expenses or asset records; and refunds to bank activity. Investigate cases where an asset is back in available inventory but its return case remains open. Sample both directions: from the ledger to case evidence and from returned-asset cases to ledger treatment.

## Check downtime without inventing a recovery

Damage can also remove equipment from the rentable fleet. Record the out-of-service start, repair authorization, return-to-service confirmation, and linked work order so operations can see the timeline. Keep any proposed downtime charge outside the customer invoice until an authorized owner approves it under the applicable agreement. A calendar calculation is not evidence that the customer owes lost rental income.

When an internal mechanic completes the work, preserve the approved parts issue and labor record without estimating a vendor invoice that does not exist. If a warranty provider or insurer may reimburse the repair, open a separately identified recovery item. Do not reduce the repair cost merely because recovery is expected. Close that item only with a remittance, credit, denial, or other authorized disposition.

Close each case with a dated summary of facts, approvals, financial movements, remaining warranty or insurance follow-up, and final links. Measure inspection lag, unresolved-deposit age, estimate-to-invoice variance, and reopened cases as control signals—not promises about loss reduction.
