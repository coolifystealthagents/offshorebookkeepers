Petty cash is small by design, which is why it is easy to stop tracking. A few receipts go missing, a purchase is recorded as "supplies," and by month end the fund is short with no clear explanation. The amount is small, but the control gap is not.
A petty cash receipt log keeps the fund reviewable without turning it into a second accounts payable process. It records each movement, links the receipt, and reconciles the count before the fund is replenished.

## Define the fund and the custodian

Record the fund amount, the account it belongs to, the custodian, and the backup. A fund without a named custodian is a drawer that anyone can use. The custodian is responsible for the count and the receipts, not for approving the spend.
State the purpose of the fund, such as small office purchases or urgent site expenses. A purpose that is too broad invites personal spending. A purpose that is too narrow sends routine purchases through the reimbursement process unnecessarily.

## Record every movement with the same fields

Use one row for each movement: date, amount, purpose, vendor, receipt link, requester, approver, and running balance. The purpose should be specific enough to review later. "Supplies" is weaker than "printer paper for the front desk."
Record the cash count at the same time. A log that records spending without the count cannot show whether the physical cash matches. The count is the control, and the log is the explanation.

## Keep the receipt with the entry

Link each entry to a receipt image or the original paper receipt. If the receipt is missing, mark it as missing and record who is following up. Do not estimate the amount to make the log balance.
For a very small purchase where the business allows an alternative record, use the approved form and mark the exception. The exception should be rare and visible. A pattern of exceptions is a signal that the fund rule needs to change.

## Separate approval from custody

The person who holds the cash should not be the only person who approves the spend. For a small fund, the manager can approve the replenishment while the custodian records the purchases. The separation protects both people.
Record the approver for each replenishment, not only for the original purchase. A replenishment is a cash movement and needs its own approval. The record should show the amount requested, the receipts attached, and the approver.

## Reconcile before replenishment

Count the remaining cash and compare it with the log balance before requesting a replenishment. Investigate any difference before adding cash. A short fund that is replenished without a check hides the error.
Record the count, the expected balance, the difference, and the resolution. If the difference is unresolved, keep the replenishment open and route the question to the manager. Do not treat a new replenishment as a correction.

## Review the pattern monthly

At month end, review the fund for missing receipts, repeated vendors, and purchases that should be on account. A recurring coffee purchase for a meeting may belong on a card rather than in petty cash. The pattern is more useful than a single entry.
Change one part of the routine based on the review. A clearer purpose field or a weekly count is often enough. Record the change and the date.

## Protect the record and the cash

Keep the log in the approved system and the cash in a locked location. Limit access to the custodian and the backup. A log that is shared broadly and a drawer that is open are both control failures.
Set a retention period with the business's records policy. Keep the receipts long enough to support the tax and audit requirements that apply. Remove copies that are no longer needed.

## Keep the sample honest

A review of one month and one fund is a check, not a benchmark. Say so in the summary. Include the fund size and the number of movements so the reader can judge the evidence.
A small fund can show a pattern but cannot support a claim about every location. Repeat the review before a conclusion is treated as stable, and state the limits in the same document.

## Key takeaways

- Name the custodian and the backup, and define the fund's purpose.
- Record each movement with a receipt and a specific purpose.
- Separate approval from custody and reconcile before replenishment.
- Review monthly for missing receipts and purchases that belong on account.

A petty cash log is a small control, but it protects the month-end close from a surprising shortfall. The discipline is in the count, not in the size of the fund. A custodian who counts before replenishing catches the missing receipt while the purchase is still recent. That small habit is what keeps the fund from becoming a write-off. A written rule for missing receipts also protects the custodian when a purchase cannot be documented. The rule should say who reviews the exception and how it is recorded.
