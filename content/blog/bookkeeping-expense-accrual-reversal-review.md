August 23, 2026 operating guide. This article is about bookkeeping preparation and review support. It is not accounting, tax, legal, payroll, or investment advice; client policy and authorized professionals control protected decisions.

## Start with the original accrual

An expense accrual reversal should point back to the accrual that created it. Record the entity, account, period, amount, source, preparer, and reviewer for the original entry. Then connect the reversal date and journal reference. A bookkeeper can build this chain from the ledger and close workpaper. The authorized finance reviewer decides whether the original estimate was still supported and whether a replacement entry is needed. If the source cannot be found, mark the reversal as unsupported rather than treating the system-generated entry as proof. This simple rule prevents a recurring accrual from disappearing into a sequence of equal and opposite journals.

## Check the period boundary

Compare the service date, invoice date, receipt date, approval date, and posting date where available. These dates answer different questions. A vendor invoice posted in the new month may relate to work completed in the prior month, while an invoice dated earlier may concern a future service period. The bookkeeper can map those observable dates and flag a conflict. The reviewer applies the company’s cutoff policy and decides the accounting treatment. Do not use one date as a substitute for all others. Keep the source reference beside each date so a reviewer can test the conclusion without asking the preparer to repeat the search.

## Look for the replacement record

After a reversal, search for the expected invoice, bill, receipt, contract, or approved estimate. Record whether the replacement was found, partially found, or absent. A match should explain the amount and service period, not merely share a vendor name. If the replacement differs, describe the difference and route it. Bookkeeping support can compare the records and calculate the variance. It should not silently edit the reversal or create an accrual to make the ledger balance. A useful queue has separate states for evidence found, evidence incomplete, reviewer decision needed, and closed with reference. That vocabulary keeps “searched” from being confused with “resolved.”

## Investigate recurring reversals

Repeated accruals and reversals often indicate a process problem rather than a single missing invoice. Group the history by vendor, account, service type, and period. Note whether the amount is stable, increasing, or changing with the underlying activity. A remote bookkeeper can prepare that history and highlight patterns. A controller or other authorized reviewer decides whether the pattern calls for a different source, a standing schedule, a policy change, or no change. Do not infer a forecast from a short history. Use the pattern to ask a better question, then preserve the answer and effective date.

## Keep proposed entries outside the source

The original journal, invoice, and supporting document should remain intact. Put a proposed amount, proposed account, and reviewer question in the workpaper rather than overwriting source values. If a correction is approved, record the posted journal reference and the person who approved it. The bookkeeper may draft an entry only when the written workflow permits drafting. Approval, unusual estimates, tax positions, and classification judgments stay with the authorized finance role. This distinction also helps a second reviewer see what was known at preparation time. A clean history is more useful than a workpaper that appears finished because it has been edited to match the final answer.

## Route exceptions with consequences

The escalation should state what happens if the question remains unanswered. For a missing invoice, the consequence may be an unsupported balance at close. For a service-period conflict, the consequence may be a cutoff decision. For a recurring variance, the consequence may be a revised review step. Include the owner, deadline, evidence checked, and permitted options. Avoid vague requests such as “please advise.” A bookkeeper working across time zones can maintain reminders and update the queue, but cannot convert a missed response into approval. Age the item from first discovery and retain the reminder history.

## Test the reversal report

Sample reversals from different accounts and periods. For each, ask whether the original accrual is present, the reversal is linked, the replacement search is documented, and the final reviewer decision is clear. Include at least one reversal with a partial replacement and one with no invoice. If another preparer cannot reproduce the search, improve the instructions or source naming. Report first-pass completion, missing links, repeat exceptions, and review waiting time. Do not judge the process by the number of reversals cleared. The test should tell management whether the ledger has a defensible explanation for the movement.

## Record the reviewer’s reasoning

When the reviewer accepts, rejects, or carries an item, preserve the reason in enough detail for the next period. A short note should identify the evidence used, the policy question answered, and any follow-up owner. The bookkeeper can format that note and connect it to the journal or invoice. It should not rewrite the reviewer’s conclusion as a fact that existed before review. This history helps distinguish a deliberate carry-forward from a missed follow-up and gives the next preparer a starting point that does not depend on a private conversation.

## Maintain the close procedure

Update the procedure when the accounting system, approval matrix, vendor process, or close calendar changes. Give the change an effective date and identify the owner. Keep the old instruction available for prior periods so a reviewer can understand which rule applied. Offshore bookkeeping support becomes dependable when the workpaper, queue, and review boundary remain stable even as people change. Expense accrual reversals are not self-explaining. A short source-linked review can show what was estimated, what cleared, what remains uncertain, and who owns the judgment.
