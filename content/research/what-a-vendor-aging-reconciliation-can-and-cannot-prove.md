A vendor aging reconciliation compares the vendor's statement with the ledger's open items. When the totals match, the reviewer marks the reconciliation complete. The match is a useful checkpoint, but it is not a guarantee that the payables record is complete or accurate.

A zero variance can hide a shared error: both reports may omit the same invoice, use the same cut-off, or classify a credit the same wrong way. The reconciliation proves agreement, not truth. A careful review treats the match as a starting point.

## What the reconciliation can show

The reconciliation can show whether the vendor's total and the ledger total agree for a defined period and vendor population. It can show the items that make up the difference when they do not.

Those facts support a review of the matching process. A recurring difference with one vendor may point to a cut-off or document issue. A difference across many vendors may point to a ledger problem.

## What the reconciliation cannot show

The reconciliation cannot show whether both records are complete. It cannot show whether an invoice is valid or whether a payment was applied to the right account.

It also cannot show whether the vendor's statement is accurate. A statement can contain errors, and a match can be built on the same error.

## Methodology

This brief reviews public guidance on internal control, records, and access, and maps it to a private vendor reconciliation. The sources support documentation and review discipline. They do not prescribe a reconciliation format or measure payables accuracy. The proposed checks are an operating design.

## Define the population and the cut-off

List the vendors and the period before comparing the totals. A reconciliation that includes one report's full vendor list and the other's partial list will produce a difference that has nothing to do with the ledger.

Record the cut-off for both reports. A statement dated the 30th and a ledger run on the 31st may include different transactions. The cut-off is part of the reconciliation, not an afterthought.

## Separate the balance types

Separate invoices, credits, prepayments, and disputed items. A total that nets a credit against an invoice can match while the underlying items are wrong. The reconciliation should show the composition, not only the net.

A credit balance deserves its own review. An unapplied credit can hide a missing invoice or a duplicate payment.

## Check the exceptions, not only the variance

A zero variance with a long exception list is not a clean reconciliation. The exception list should show the items that were adjusted, the reason, and the approval.

A variance with no explanation is a finding. A variance with a clear timing explanation and a follow-up date is a managed item.

## Reconcile the payments

The statement and the ledger can agree on invoices and disagree on payments. Check the payment dates and the amounts, especially for vendors with multiple invoices and credits.

A payment applied to the wrong invoice can create a difference that looks like a missing document. Trace the payment before adding a new item.

## Record the reviewer and the date

The reconciliation should name the preparer and the reviewer, with the date. A reviewer who did not prepare the reconciliation adds a useful check.

A reconciliation without a reviewer is a prepared report, not a control. The signature or note is the evidence.

## Protect the vendor data

Vendor statements contain bank details and commercial terms. Keep the reconciliation in the approved system and limit access. Do not forward a full statement to a broad channel.

Set a retention period with the client's records policy.

## Check the vendor master

A reconciliation can fail because the vendor exists twice or the statement is under a different name. Check the vendor master before treating the difference as a ledger issue.

Record the canonical vendor and the duplicate. A master-data fix prevents the same reconciliation problem next month.

## Use a consistent cut-off

Both reports should use the same period. A statement dated the last day and a ledger run on the first day will disagree for legitimate timing reasons.

Record the cut-off beside the reconciliation. A later reviewer should not have to guess which period was compared.

## Decide what the review will change

Before the review, name two changes the team would make depending on the result. If cut-off errors repeat, the process may need a fixed date. If a vendor repeats a statement error, the vendor conversation may need a different contact.

Share the summary with the payables team and the vendor owner.

## Keep a short exception note

For every difference, write one line: the cause, the owner, and the next action. A reviewer who can read the note does not need to reopen the statement.

Update the note when the cause changes.

## Write the outcome down

End the review with a short note: the vendor, the period, the variance, the exceptions, and the one or two changes the team will try. A written outcome keeps the next review from starting over.

## Keep the queue visible

A reconciliation that is filed away after the match is hard to review later. Keep the current queue and the open exceptions in a shared view. The next reviewer can see what is still unresolved.

The view should be simple: vendor, period, difference, cause, owner, date. A longer report is not needed for the review.

## Keep the process proportionate

A small vendor list does not need a formal reconciliation every month. A quarterly review with a clear cut-off may be enough. Match the cadence to the volume and the risk.

The record should say which cadence applies. A reader should not assume a monthly process when the team runs a quarterly one.

## Keep the record ready for a dispute

If a vendor later disputes a balance, the reconciliation should show what was compared, when, and by whom. A short folder per vendor with the statement, the ledger extract, and the resolution note is enough.

A later reviewer should not have to rebuild the comparison from email. The record is the evidence that the work happened.

## Bounded pilot

Reconcile one vendor for one month. Record the population, the cut-off, the composition, the exceptions, and the reviewer. Ask a colleague who did not prepare it to explain what the reconciliation shows and what it does not. If they cannot separate a match from completeness, the reconciliation needs clearer fields.

## Limitations

This brief describes an operating review model. It does not establish an audit opinion, a legal standard, or a measure of payables accuracy. The cited sources support general control and records principles. Results depend on the reports and the completeness of the underlying records.

## Conclusion

A vendor aging reconciliation is an agreement check, not proof that the payables are correct. It becomes useful when the population, the cut-off, the composition, and the exceptions are reviewed. That discipline turns a matched total into a record the team can trust.
