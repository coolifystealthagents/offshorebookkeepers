Month-end close checklists are popular because they make a complex process look orderly. A list of steps, a set of tick boxes, and a completion date give the impression that the books are controlled. The impression is partly right: a checklist can show that a step was assigned and marked done. It cannot show that the result was correct, complete, or reviewed by someone with authority.

The distinction matters when a business hands bookkeeping to an offshore team or changes providers. The new team inherits the checklist and may treat it as proof that the prior close was sound. A short review of what the checklist actually records will prevent that assumption from becoming a problem.

## What a checklist can show

A checklist can show that a named person performed a defined step within a stated period. It can show that a reconciliation was prepared, a bank account was reviewed, or a journal entry was posted. It can also show that a reviewer signed off, provided the signature is specific and dated.

Those are useful facts. They support a conversation about whether the process was followed. They do not, by themselves, support a conclusion about the numbers. A bank reconciliation can be prepared and still contain an unreconciled difference. A journal entry can be posted to the wrong period. The checklist records the action, not the quality of the outcome.

## What a checklist cannot show

A checklist cannot show that every material account was covered. It cannot show that a judgment was reasonable or that an estimate was supported. It cannot show that a reviewer actually read the reconciliation rather than signing a list. And it cannot show that an exception was identified and resolved.

These limits are not a reason to abandon checklists. They are a reason to pair the checklist with evidence that tests the result. A reconciliation with a support file, a sample of coded transactions, and a named reviewer are stronger than a tick mark.

## Methodology and sources

## Separate completion from review

For each material step, record two facts: who performed it and who reviewed it. If the same person did both, say so. A small team may not be able to separate the roles, but the checklist should not imply an independent review that did not happen.

The reviewer should record what they checked, not only that they checked. A short note such as "agreed bank balance to statement, cleared three timing items, one unresolved" is more useful than a signature. It gives the next reviewer a starting point and shows that the work was examined.

## Test completeness with exceptions

A checklist that is fully ticked can still be incomplete. Add a small exception report: accounts with no activity, reconciliations with unresolved differences, journals posted after the close date, and accounts that were not reviewed. The exception list is often more informative than the completion rate.

Sample a few transactions from each material account and trace them to support. This is not a full audit, and it should not be presented as one. It is a check that the checklist's claims are consistent with the underlying records.

## Name the reviewer and the authority

The close should have a named reviewer with authority for the entity or business unit. That person should review the exceptions and the material reconciliations, not only the checklist. If the reviewer is remote, record how the review was performed and what evidence was available.

When authority changes, record the handoff. A new reviewer who inherits a close without a note may repeat work or accept an unresolved item. The handoff record is part of the close evidence, not an administrative extra.

## State the scope and exclusions

Every close checklist should state what it covers: which entity, which accounts, which period, and which judgments are outside the close. Tax filings, payroll compliance, and management reporting may sit in other processes. A reader should not assume the checklist covers them because the list is long. Name the person who owns each excluded process so the boundary has a contact.

The scope statement also protects the bookkeeping team. If a question falls outside the stated scope, the team can point to the boundary rather than absorb an unrelated task at the last minute. Write the scope in the same document as the checklist so it travels with the work.

## Review the checklist itself

Once a quarter, review the checklist for steps that no longer matter and accounts that have grown material. A checklist that has not changed in two years may be missing a new revenue stream or a new bank account. Remove steps that no longer produce a decision, and add steps that address current risk. Ask the person who performs the steps whether any item is ambiguous or duplicated.

Keep a version date on the checklist. A close performed under an old version should be identifiable, especially if the business later compares periods or changes providers. Note who approved the change and when.

## What evidence to attach to each step

For a reconciliation step, attach the reconciliation file, the statement it was agreed to, and a note of any unresolved difference. For a journal entry step, attach the entry number, the support, and the approver. For a review step, attach the reviewer's note, not only a signature. These attachments turn a checklist into an evidence pack that a successor can inspect.

The evidence should be stored where access can be controlled. A link to a shared drive is acceptable only if the permissions are appropriate and the link will still work for the reviewer. A checklist that points to a file that has moved is not complete, even if the box is ticked. Test a few links before treating the pack as finished.

## Bounded pilot

Take one recent close and test three things: whether each material account has a reconciliation with support, whether a reviewer other than the preparer signed off, and whether exceptions were listed and resolved. Do not re-perform the entire close. The goal is to see whether the checklist's claims are supported by evidence, and to identify the gaps that matter.

## Limitations

This brief describes an operating review model. It does not establish an audit opinion, a legal standard, or a universal close checklist. The cited sources provide general control and recordkeeping principles. Results depend on the entity, the accounting system, and the quality of the underlying records.

## Conclusion

A month-end close checklist is a useful coordination tool, not proof that the close is accurate. It becomes credible when paired with named reviewers, reconciliation support, exception reporting, and a stated scope. That combination gives the next bookkeeper something better than a row of tick marks. It also gives the business a defensible record if a later question arises about a balance or a journal entry.
