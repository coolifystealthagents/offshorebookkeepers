A register holds 60 assets, including three laptops that were sold two years ago, a copier listed twice, and a fully depreciated server still carried at a small value. The cleanup removes the obvious duplicates and the register looks tidy. The depreciation schedule still uses the wrong life for the new equipment.

A fixed asset register cleanup removes and corrects records. This brief separates the cleanup, the existence check, and the accounting policy, and explains what the register can support.

## Define the cleanup scope

Record the period covered, the categories included, and the source of the asset list. A cleanup limited to the current year leaves the older duplicates in place.

State whether the cleanup includes the ledger tie-out and the physical sample. A register edit without those steps is a tidy list.

## Classify the entries

Use a short list: active, disposed, missing, duplicate, and fully depreciated but retained. Each category needs a different action and approval.

A missing asset is not automatically a disposal. Record the search and the decision before removing it.

## What the cleanup can show

The cleanup can show that duplicates were merged, disposals recorded, and the register tied to the ledger. It can show the assets that need a physical check.

That is enough to make the register usable for reporting and insurance review.

## What the cleanup cannot show

The cleanup cannot show that an asset exists, that its location is right, or that its useful life is correct.

It also cannot show that the disposal was authorized. The approval is a separate record, and it matters at audit time.

## Tie the register to the ledger

Reconcile the register total to the fixed asset accounts. Record the difference and the adjustment for each category.

A register that does not tie to the ledger is a list with no authority. The tie-out is the first control.

## Sample the physical assets

Select a sample across locations and categories and verify the asset tag, the location, and the condition. Record the result.

The sample is what separates a cleanup from an asset count. A tidy register with missing laptops is still wrong.

## Check the useful lives

Review the depreciation life for the new categories. A register cleanup that preserves an incorrect life keeps the expense wrong every month.

Record the policy source and the change. The adjustment belongs to the accountant and the owner, not the cleanup alone.

## A worked example

A cleanup starts with 60 entries. It merges two duplicates, records four disposals with approval, and flags five assets for a physical check.

The register ties to the ledger and the sample finds four of the five. The fifth is removed after a documented search. The depreciation life for the new equipment is corrected.

## Method note and limits

This brief is an operating design informed by public guidance on internal control and recordkeeping. It does not report an empirical study or give tax advice.

The fields were tested against common asset patterns: office equipment, vehicles, leasehold improvements, and technology refresh. The design keeps cleanup, existence, and policy separate.

## Conclusion

A fixed asset register cleanup proves the list was edited, not that the assets exist. Tying it to the ledger, sampling the assets, and checking the lives turns the cleanup into an accurate register.
