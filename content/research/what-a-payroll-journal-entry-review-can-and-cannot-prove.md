A payroll journal entry balances and the close moves on. The wages, the taxes, and the benefits all have a line. Whether the amounts match the payroll register is a different question, and the balance does not answer it.
A payroll journal entry review checks the entry against the payroll register and the payment. It is useful because payroll is a high-value, high-risk cycle. It is also easy to overclaim, because a balanced entry is not an accurate payroll.

## What the review can show

The review can show the payroll register, the journal entry, the bank payment, and the tax filing. It can show the differences and the reviewer. It can show the cycles that were reviewed and the ones that were not.
Those facts support a review of the payroll process. A cluster of mapping differences may indicate a chart-of-accounts problem. A cluster of unreviewed cycles may indicate a capacity gap.

## What the review cannot show

The review cannot show whether every employee was paid correctly. It cannot show whether the hours or the rates were right. It also cannot show whether the payroll complied with every rule.
The review cannot prove that the payroll is free of error. It shows that the entry and the register were compared. The detail is in the payroll records.

## Methodology

We reviewed public guidance on internal control, recordkeeping, and wage and hour administration, and mapped it to a private payroll review. The sources support documentation and review discipline. They do not prescribe a payroll process or a tax treatment.
We tested the model against four common cases: a payroll that matches, a mapping difference, a missed payment, and a tax filing difference. The proposed fields are an operating design.

## Record the register and the entry

Record the payroll cycle, the register total, the journal entry total, and the payment total. A review without the three amounts cannot show where the difference sits.
If the totals differ, record the difference and the account. The difference may be a mapping issue, a timing issue, or an error. The review should name the cause and the owner.

## Separate the payment from the entry

A journal entry can be correct while the payment is wrong. Record the bank payment and the date. Compare the payment to the register, not only to the entry.
If the payment failed or was returned, record the correction. A payroll that was entered but not paid is an open item. The review should show the state.

## Check the tax filing

Record the tax filing amount and the date. Compare it with the register and the entry. A difference may be a timing issue or a rate change. The review should name the cause.
If the filing is late, record the date and the reason. A late filing is a compliance risk. The review should show the owner and the correction.

## Review the mapping

Record the accounts used for wages, taxes, and benefits. A mapping difference may move the cost to the wrong period or department. The review should show the mapping and the change.
If the mapping changes, record the effective date and the approver. A mapping that changes quietly makes the trend hard to read. The review should name the version.

## Keep the review evidence

Record the reviewer, the date, and the evidence. A review that is not recorded is hard to distinguish from one that was never done. The evidence should point to the register and the payment.
If the reviewer is unavailable, record the alternate route. The review should not wait indefinitely. The record should show the decision.

## Protect the payroll record

The review can include names, amounts, and tax detail. Keep it in the payroll system and limit access to the people who need it. Do not circulate the payroll register in a broad channel.
Set a retention period with the business's records policy. Keep enough to support an audit and no more. A payroll review log kept indefinitely creates risk without adding insight.

## Keep the review light enough to use

The review should take a few minutes per cycle, not a full audit. Record the register, the entry, the payment, and the tax filing. Leave the employee detail in the payroll system. Review the fields after a month and remove any that nobody used.

## Keep the sample honest

A review of one month and one entity is a check, not a benchmark. Say so in the summary. Include the cycle count and the difference types so the reader can judge the evidence.
A small sample can show a pattern but cannot support a percentage for every entity. Repeat the review before a conclusion is treated as stable, and state the limits in the same document.

## Review the payroll register

The register is the source for the employee detail. Record the total wages, taxes, and deductions. Compare the register with the entry and the payment. A difference should have an owner.

## Keep the review light

The review should take a few minutes, not a day. Record the register, the entry, the payment, and the filing. Leave the detail in the payroll system. Review the fields after a month.

## Review the tax filing timing

Record when the filing is due and when it was made. A late filing is a compliance risk. The review should show the owner and the correction. A simple calendar is enough.

## Review the mapping changes

Record the mapping for wages, taxes, and benefits. A change should have a date and an approver. The review should use the mapping that applied to the period. A quiet change makes the trend hard to read.

## Keep the payment evidence

Record the bank payment and the date. A payroll that was entered but not paid is an open item. The evidence should point to the payment record. A short link is enough to make the payment reviewable. It should point to the exact date and amount.

## Review the differences with payroll

Bring the differences to the payroll owner. Ask whether the cause is mapping, timing, or error. The conversation should produce one or two changes. Record the change and the date.

## Bounded pilot

Review one month of payroll cycles for one entity. Record the register, the entry, the payment, and the tax filing. Report the differences and the open items. Ask a reviewer who did not process payroll to trace three entries to their payments. If they cannot, the fields need work.

## Limitations

This brief describes an operating review model. It does not establish a payroll standard, a tax treatment, or a legal obligation. The cited sources support general control and records principles. Results depend on the completeness of the register and the accuracy of the payments.

## Conclusion

A payroll journal entry review shows that the entry and the register were compared, not that every payment was right. It becomes useful when the entry, the payment, the filing, and the reviewer are recorded together. That discipline makes the payroll close explainable.
