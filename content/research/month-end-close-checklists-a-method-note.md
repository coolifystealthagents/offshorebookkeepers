A close checklist has 60 items. The bookkeeper works through them in order, the close takes nine days, and two of the reconciliations are done before the bank feed is complete. The list was followed; the sequence was wrong.

A month-end close checklist is a useful control and a weak proof of accuracy. This method note describes how to build one that fits a small team.

## Order by dependency

Start with the steps that unlock others: bank feeds, cutoffs, and the reconciliations that depend on them. A report that needs complete data should not be prepared first.

Record the dependency next to the step. A checklist without dependencies is a list, not a process.

## Give every step an owner and a date

A step without an owner is a hope. Record the person and the target date for each item.

The date should be realistic for the team's volume. A checklist that assumes a five-day close for a two-person team will fail every month.

## Attach the evidence

Each step should produce a file, a reconciliation, or a note. Record where the evidence is stored.

A tick without evidence is a claim. The evidence is what a reviewer or an auditor will ask for.

## Separate preparation from review

The person who prepares a reconciliation should not be the only reviewer. Add a review step with a named reviewer.

Record the review and the date. The second look is the control that catches the error the preparer missed.

## What the checklist can show

The checklist can show that the steps ran and who performed them. It can show the evidence and the review.

It can support a faster close over time. The timing data shows where the process waits.

## What the checklist cannot show

The checklist cannot show that the ledger is accurate. A completed list with a wrong invoice is still wrong.

It cannot show that the review was meaningful. A reviewer who ticks without looking is a step, not a control.

It also cannot show that the close was timely for the client's decisions. That depends on the reporting date and the use.

## Methodology and limits

This note is an operating design informed by public guidance on internal control, recordkeeping, and small-business operations. It does not report an empirical study or prescribe a close calendar.

The fields were tested against common closes: a simple service business, a retail business with inventory, a payroll-heavy business, and a multi-entity client. The design keeps the dependency and the evidence visible.

## Tooling

A checklist can live in a shared sheet, the accounting system, or a project tool. The tool matters less than the owner and the evidence.

Record the location and the access. A checklist that only one person can open is a single point of failure.

## Measure the close

Record the start and end dates and the steps that caused a delay. The pattern is more useful than the total.

A close that always waits on the same client document is a client communication issue, not a bookkeeping one.

## Review the checklist quarterly

Remove the steps that no longer apply and add the ones the team does from memory. Record the change.

A checklist that grows without review becomes a document nobody reads. The quarterly edit keeps it useful.

## A worked example

A close has 24 steps. The review shows that the bank reconciliation waits for a statement that arrives on day three, and the report is prepared on day one.

The checklist is reordered, and the close shortens by two days. The evidence and the review are unchanged.

## Conclusion

A month-end close checklist shows the steps, the owners, and the evidence. Used with dependencies and a second review, it makes the close repeatable. Used as a tick list, it records activity without proving accuracy.
