A vendor sends a certificate of insurance with a date six months in the future. The bookkeeping team adds the expiry to a log and moves on. When the date arrives, a reminder goes out. The log has done its job.

What the log has not done is confirm that the coverage was in force, that the limits were adequate, or that the policy covered the work. A certificate is a snapshot, often issued by a broker, and it can be out of date the day after it is issued. The log is a reminder tool, not proof of coverage.

## What the log can show

The log can show the certificate on file, the expiry date, the vendor, and the date the document was received. It can show which vendors are due for renewal and which have not responded.

Those facts support a renewal process. A vendor with a certificate expiring soon can be contacted early. A vendor with no certificate on file can be flagged before the next job.

## What the log cannot show

The log cannot show whether the policy is active, whether the premium is paid, or whether the coverage applies to the specific work. It cannot show whether the limits are adequate for the risk.

The log also cannot show whether the certificate is authentic. A document can be altered, and a copy can be stale. Verification with the issuer is a separate step.

## Methodology

This brief reviews public guidance on internal control, records, and risk management, and maps it to a private vendor certificate process. The sources support documentation and review discipline. They do not prescribe insurance limits or replace a broker's advice. The proposed fields are an operating design.

## Record more than the expiry date

For each certificate, record the type, the named insured, the policy number, the limits, the effective date, the expiry date, the issuer, and the date received. Add the verification method and the date.

A log with only an expiry date cannot answer the questions that matter when a claim arises. The additional fields make the record useful beyond the reminder.

## Verify high-risk vendors

For high-risk work, verify the certificate with the broker or the issuer using a contact from the existing vendor record. Confirm the policy is active, the limits are current, and the named insured matches the contract.

Record who verified the certificate and when. The verification is a stronger control than the document alone, and it should be visible in the file.

## Separate the certificate types

General liability, workers' compensation, auto, and professional liability expire on different schedules and carry different limits. Track each type separately so a renewal reminder does not assume that one certificate covers everything.

A vendor may have a current general liability certificate and an expired auto policy. A combined log with one expiry date would miss that gap.

## Handle the renewal before expiry

Start the renewal process before the expiry date, not on it. The lead time should match the vendor's history and the risk of the work. A vendor that consistently sends documents late needs an earlier request.

If the certificate expires and a new one has not arrived, decide whether the vendor can continue. That decision belongs to the manager and should be recorded, not left to the bookkeeper.

## Review the log monthly

Look at the certificates due in the next sixty days, the vendors with no certificate, and the verifications that are overdue. A short monthly review keeps the renewals from becoming urgent.

Report the count and the exceptions rather than a simple percentage. A high completion rate can hide the one vendor who matters for a high-risk job.

## Protect the vendor's information

Certificates contain policy numbers, limits, and contact details. Keep them in the approved system and limit access to the people who need them. Do not forward a certificate to a broad group.

Set a retention period with the client's records policy. A certificate archive kept indefinitely creates risk without adding insight.

## Use the log with the contract

The certificate should be read beside the contract. The contract says what coverage is required; the certificate says what the vendor has. A certificate that meets the contract is a stronger position than one that is merely current.

If the certificate does not match the contract, record the gap and route it to the manager. Do not accept a substitute document because the expiry date is in the future.

## Keep the reminder simple

A reminder should name the vendor, the certificate, the expiry, and the action. Send it to the contact who can produce the document, and copy the manager only when the deadline is close.

A reminder that goes to a general inbox is easy to ignore. A named contact and a specific request are more likely to get a response.

## Decide what the review will change

Before the review, name two changes the business would make depending on the result. If renewals are often late, the business might start the request earlier. If verifications are often missing, the business might add a broker check for high-risk work.

Share the summary with the vendor contacts and the manager. Both see different parts of the process and can suggest a fix that keeps the renewals on time.

## Keep the records together

Store the certificate, the verification note, and the contract reference in one place. A certificate that is easy to find but disconnected from the contract is only half a record. The next reviewer should be able to see the requirement and the evidence together.

If the vendor changes its broker or policy, keep the prior certificate in the history. The change may explain a gap or a limit difference that would otherwise be confusing.

## Use a simple status

A status of current, expiring, expired, or missing is enough for most vendors. Add a separate verification status for high-risk work. The simple status keeps the log readable, while the verification field keeps it honest.

Review the status at the same cadence as the renewals. A log that is updated only when someone asks is already behind.

## Bounded pilot

Review one month of certificates. Check the fields, the expiry dates, and the verifications for high-risk vendors. Contact one broker to confirm a certificate and record the result. Ask a reviewer who did not maintain the log to explain what it shows and what it does not. If they cannot separate a reminder from a verification, the log needs clearer fields.

## Limitations

This brief describes an operating review model. It does not establish insurance requirements, a legal standard, or a coverage opinion. The cited sources support general control and records principles. Results depend on the quality of the certificates and the verification process.

## Conclusion

A vendor certificate expiry log is a reminder system, not proof of coverage. It becomes useful when the certificate type, limits, and verification are recorded beside the expiry. That discipline starts renewals early and gives the manager a clear basis for deciding whether a vendor can continue, rather than relying on a date alone.
