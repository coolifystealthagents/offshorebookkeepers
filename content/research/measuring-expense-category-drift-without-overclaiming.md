A software subscription is coded to office expense in January and to professional fees in March. The total spend is the same, the reports look different, and the category comparison becomes unreliable. The coding drifted; the spending did not.
Measuring expense category drift is useful because category reports drive budgets and tax treatment. It is also easy to overclaim, because a changed category is not always a changed cost.

## What the measure can show

The measure can show the transaction, the category, the period, and the coder. It can show the categories that changed and the rules used. It can show the transactions that were recoded.
Those facts support a review of the coding process. A cluster of changes in one category may indicate a rule change. A cluster in one team may indicate a training gap.

## What the measure cannot show

The measure cannot show whether the new category is correct. It cannot show whether the spend changed. It also cannot show whether the vendor or the description changed.
The measure cannot prove that the books are wrong. A category change may be a correction. The rule and the reviewer matter before a conclusion is drawn.

## Methodology

We reviewed public guidance on internal control, recordkeeping, and small-business operations, and mapped it to a private expense process. The sources support documentation and review discipline. They do not prescribe a category rule or a review threshold.
We tested the model against four common cases: a transaction coded differently after a rule change, a transaction recoded after review, a transaction coded differently by mistake, and a real change in spending. The proposed fields are an operating design.

## Write the coding rule

Write the rule for common transactions before the review. Include examples such as software, travel, and professional fees. A rule that exists only in the bookkeeper's memory will drift.
Record the rule version and the date. If the rule changes, record the change and the effective date. The review should use the rule that applied to the period.

## Record the transaction and the category

Record the vendor, the description, the amount, the category, and the period. A category without the transaction is hard to verify. The description should be specific.
If the category changed, record the old and new values and the reason. A recode without a reason looks like an error. The review should show the decision.

## Separate the rule change from the error

A rule change is deliberate; an error is not. Record the rule version and the reviewer. A change that follows a new rule is not a drift.
If the coder was unsure, record the question and the answer. A recurring question may indicate that the rule needs an example. The review should name the fix.

## Review the pattern by category

Group the drift by category and by period. A category that changes once may be a correction. A category that changes repeatedly may be a mapping problem. The pattern is more useful than a single transaction.
Share the pattern with the bookkeeper and the manager. The conversation should produce one or two changes. Record the change and the date.

## Check the report impact

Record the effect on the category report and the budget. A drift that moves cost between categories can change the story even when the total is the same. The review should show the impact.
If the report is used for a tax or grant return, record the version and the correction. A category change that affects a filing needs a reviewer. The record should show the decision.

## Protect the expense record

The review can include vendor names, amounts, and descriptions. Keep it in the accounting system and limit access. Do not circulate a category list in a broad channel.
Set a retention period with the business's records policy. Keep enough to support a review and no more. A drift log kept indefinitely creates risk without adding insight.

## Keep the review light enough to use

The review should take a minute per transaction, not a full audit. Record the vendor, the amount, the category, and the reason. Leave the receipt in the expense system. Review the fields after a month and remove any that nobody used.

## Keep the sample honest

A review of one month and one entity is a check, not a benchmark. Say so in the summary. Include the transaction count and the categories so the reader can judge the evidence.
A small sample can show a pattern but cannot support a percentage for every entity. Repeat the review before a conclusion is treated as stable, and state the limits in the same document.

## Review the category examples

A rule with examples is easier to apply than a rule with definitions. Add a short example for each common expense. Review the examples after a quarter. A clear example reduces drift.

## Review the rule with the bookkeeper

Ask the bookkeeper which transaction is hardest to code. The answer shows where the rule needs an example. Record the feedback and change one field. A short conversation is enough.

## Review the report impact

Record the effect of a recode on the category report. A move between categories can change the story even when the total is the same. The review should show the impact and the correction. A short note on the impact is enough to start the conversation.

## Keep the transaction evidence

Record the vendor, the amount, and the receipt link. A category without the transaction is hard to verify. A short link is enough to make the category reviewable. It should point to the exact vendor and period. Keep the detail in the expense system.

## Review the drift with the manager

Bring the changed categories to the manager review. Ask whether the rule or the training needs a change. The conversation should produce one or two changes. Record the change and the date.

## Bounded pilot

Review one month of expense coding for one entity. Record the transaction, the category, the rule, and the reason. Report the changes and the open items. Ask a reviewer who did not code the expenses to trace three categories to the rule. If they cannot, the fields need work.

## Limitations

This brief describes an operating review model. It does not establish a tax standard, a legal obligation, or a measure of coding quality. The cited sources support general control and records principles. Results depend on the completeness of the transactions and the clarity of the rule.

## Conclusion

Expense category drift shows a change in coding, not always a change in spending. It becomes useful when the rule, the transaction, and the reason are recorded together. That discipline keeps the category reports comparable.
