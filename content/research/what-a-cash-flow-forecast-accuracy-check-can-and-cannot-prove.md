A cash flow forecast predicts a 40,000-dollar closing balance. The month closes at 39,600, and the forecast is called accurate. The number matched because a late customer payment offset an early payroll run, and the business was closer to a squeeze than the total suggested.

A forecast accuracy check is a useful discipline and a weak proof of control. This brief examines what the check can show, what it hides, and how to report it.

## Defining accuracy

Accuracy can be the difference in the closing balance, the difference by week, or the difference by category. Each measures a different thing.

Write the definition down. A check that looks only at the month end will miss the timing errors that matter most.

## What the check can show

The check can show the size and direction of the forecast error. It can show the categories that are consistently over or under.

It can support a better forecast. A repeated error in one category is a data or assumption problem, not a forecasting failure.

## What the check cannot show

The check cannot show whether the forecast was useful. A forecast that is wrong but prompts a useful decision is still valuable.

It cannot show whether the balance was safe. A month-end match can hide a week with no cash.

It also cannot show whether the assumptions were reasonable. That needs the assumption log and the context.

## Compare by week

A weekly comparison shows whether the cash arrived when expected. A month-end total can look accurate while the weekly path was dangerous.

Record the weekly variance and the reason. The pattern is more useful than the monthly number.

## Compare by category

Group the forecast and actual by receipts, payroll, suppliers, tax, and debt. A category that is consistently wrong is easier to fix than a total.

Record the category variance and the owner. The owner can explain the assumption.

## Check the assumption log

Record the assumptions behind the forecast and the date they changed. A forecast without an assumption log cannot be reviewed.

A change in a key assumption should be noted with the source. The check can then see whether the forecast reflected it.

## A worked example

A forecast predicts a 40,000-dollar balance. The weekly path shows a low of 2,000 in week two, and the actual low was 800.

The month-end match hides a near miss. The review focuses on the timing of the customer payment and recommends a buffer.

## Forecast horizon

A one-month forecast and a three-month forecast have different accuracy expectations. Record the horizon with the result.

A short horizon can be accurate and still leave the business unprepared for a later squeeze. The check should say what it measured.

## A worked example

A forecast predicts a 40,000-dollar month-end balance and a 2,000-dollar low in week two. The actual low is 800.

The month-end error is small, and the weekly error is large. The review recommends a buffer and a weekly cash call for the next quarter.

## Reporting cadence

Review monthly and the assumptions quarterly. Keep the definitions stable.

Record the actions and the outcomes. A check that produces no change is a report.

## Limits and appropriate use

This brief is a method note, not an empirical study. It does not establish an accuracy target. Results depend on the quality of the actuals and the honesty of the assumptions.

Small businesses may forecast only the month end, and the check should say so. The gap is itself a finding about the forecast.

## Conclusion

A forecast accuracy check shows the size and direction of the error. Used by week and by category, it shows whether the cash arrived when expected. Used only at month end, it can celebrate a match that hid a dangerous path.
